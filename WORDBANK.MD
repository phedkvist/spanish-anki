# Word bank — what the collection is still missing

A queue of words worth cards, so that any future block can pull from here
instead of being invented from scratch. Tick a line when it lands in a deck,
and note which tag it went into.

**How this list was built.** Every entry was checked against `decks/*.tsv`
before being listed. Some appear once or twice inside a card about something
else, which raises the fair question of whether that is already enough.

The test is the sense, not the count. A word inside a card you review is seen
every time that card comes up — twenty times a year is normal — and for
**recognition** that is plenty. What it does not give is **production**: on that
card you are asked to produce something else, so the word itself is never
retrieved. Card it when you need to say it unprompted, or when the existing
appearance carries a different meaning — *quedan dos días* (to be left) teaches
nothing about *quedamos a las ocho* (to arrange to meet).

By that test some entries here are weaker than others, and a word that appears
in a card meaning the same thing can simply be struck off.

**Where the collection stands.** 909 distinct Spanish word forms across the
decks — roughly 600 headwords once inflections collapse, against a usual A1
target of 500–600. So size is not the problem. Two things are: 53% of those
forms appear on exactly one card, and the missing vocabulary clusters into
whole themes rather than scattering (see *Nouns, by theme* below).

**Frequency comes from** Mark Davies, *A Frequency Dictionary of Spanish*
(Routledge, 2nd ed.), built on a 100M-word corpus. Two facts from it shape the
order below: the twelve most frequent verbs are all irregular, and the most
frequent regular verb (*llegar*) sits over halfway down the top 100. Irregular
and idiomatic verbs earn their cards; regular ones mostly look after themselves
once the pattern is known.

## Verbs — the top-frequency gaps

Five of the 24 highest-frequency verbs in the corpus have no cards. All five
are idiomatic, which is exactly why knowing the conjugation isn't enough.

- [x] **pasar** — `A1::pasar`, 3–4 cards + poster row 10 — to happen, to pass, to spend time · *¿qué pasa?* · *pasar el fin de semana*

### Place words — the *aquí / ahí / allí* three-way

The deck has *aquí, encima de, dentro de, delante de, debajo de, fuera de,
detrás de, entre, en medio de, al lado de*. The gap is the distance ladder,
which English cannot express in one word each:

- [ ] **aquí** (here, by me) · **ahí** (there, by you) · **allí** (there, away from both) · **allá** (over there, vaguer)
- [ ] **junto a** — right next to · tighter than *al lado de*
- [ ] **alrededor de** — around · already listed from the reader

The three-way maps onto *este / ese / aquel*, so one card can teach both.

### Corrections to the lists themselves

Worth fixing in the Excalidraw before any of it becomes a card, because a wrong
note becomes a wrong card that then has to be re-learned:

- **camisa and camiseta are the wrong way round.** *La camisa* is a shirt
  (skjorta); *la camiseta* is a t-shirt. Your note has them swapped.
- **The weather phrasings are calques.** *Está soleado / lluvioso / ventoso* is
  not how Spain says it: **hace sol**, **hace viento**, **está lloviendo** or
  **llueve**, **está nevando** or **nieva**. Your deck already has the correct
  forms (*hace sol y estamos a veinticinco grados*, *hay niebla y hace mucho
  viento*, *mañana va a nevar*) — the notes are the thing that's wrong here, not
  the cards. *Está nublado* is right, which is probably where the pattern came
  from.
- **Latin-American forms.** *enojarse / enojado* is LatAm; Spain says
  **enfadarse / enfadado**, and your deck already carries that note. *Lindo* is
  LatAm; Spain uses **bonito**, **mono** or **guapo**. *Chofer* is **chófer** in
  Spain, and **conductor** is more usual. *Alergista* is **alergólogo**.
- **doctor vs médico.** In Spain *médico* is the profession, *doctor* mostly the
  title you address them with. The deck has *médico*.
- **comerciante** is a trader or shopkeeper, not a businessman — that is
  *empresario* or *hombre/mujer de negocios*.
- Spelling: *despartarse* &rarr; **despertarse**, *dormise* &rarr; **dormirse**,
  *Receptionista* &rarr; **recepcionista**, *marron* &rarr; **marrón**.

### Skipped

- **analista, nutricionista, comerciante, gerente, presidente, asistente,
  alergista, chef** — profession lists pad easily. None of these is a word you
  need before B1 unless it's your own job or someone's in the class.
- **casa de alquiler** — *alquilar* and *el alquiler* are worth a card; the

## How to use this

When a block is being written — because a course lesson landed, or a gap turned
up — check here first. A block of ten built around words already on this list is
worth more than ten fresh sentences about things the deck covers three times
over.

The queue above is the ordered answer when a block is due. The groups below are
the older, un-ordered backlog, still valid but lower priority.

Order to spend on, if there's no other signal:

1. The body and weather/nature nouns — whole themes missing, and both are
   standard A1 units that a course will reach
2. The five top-frequency verbs — highest frequency, most idiomatic
3. Adjectives for people — the thinnest word class, and immediately usable
4. Connectors — they make what you can already say sound less like a textbook
5. The everyday verbs — useful, but regular and easier to absorb from input
6. The reading list — out of order on purpose: pull from it while that book
   is being read, when the words have a page behind them, not before

The class word lists are the exception to all of the above. They jump the
queue, because a lesson covering something is the best reason there is to card
it. The months are the place to start: a closed set of twelve, more than half
missing, and the course has already been there.

See `CONTENT.MD` for grammar blocks queued by course order. This file is about
vocabulary; that one is about structures.
